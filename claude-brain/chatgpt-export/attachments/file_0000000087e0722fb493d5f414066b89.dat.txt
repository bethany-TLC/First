Here is everything we covered, organized into a plan you can run this week. No fluff. Work it top to bottom.


THE SNAPSHOT

- You own 60% and run everything. Eric owns 40% and is the financial backer and CFO.
- Eric ghosted, missed multiple partner conversations, and missed the med spa network deal call. He was in rehab.
- You lacked financial access. Cards declined, GHL went down, payroll was at risk. You worked the weekend to keep it alive.
- The roles you expected from him on hiring, firing, and shared decisions never happened.
- You found out ad spend was set at roughly $1,500 a month, not the $5,000 you assumed. You have since tripled spend and calls are coming in.


THE CORE ISSUE

You do not have a money problem. You have a control and clarity problem. Eric is willing to fund. He is not reliable to operate. The fix is to separate those two roles cleanly and put it on paper.


THE STANDARD TO HOLD

Ask yourself this. If a top employee missed a call that could have signed a major med spa network, would you keep them? You would not. Hold a partner to the same bar. Anyone who damages the business is a risk to the whole company. Recent substance issues mean you cannot predict the next time. His money is welcome. His operational control is not.


PART 1: LOCK DOWN CONTROL

1. Get access to everything. Bank, Stripe, GHL, ad accounts, domains, payroll, vendor logins. Frame it simply. You need access to run the business if anything ever happens again. If he resists or refuses, treat that as a serious red flag.

2. Remove Eric from day to day operations. Position it as a win for him. You take over all operations. He carries no operational responsibility. Tell him you welcome regular check ins and his input as a sounding board, but you are running the business.


PART 2: PUT THE MONEY DEAL ON PAPER

He keeps his equity. In exchange, his financial role gets defined in writing. Loose verbal trust is what put you in this spot. Build a written agreement that covers the following.

1. Define his funding obligation with a cap. He commits to cover operating shortfalls up to a set amount per quarter or per year. He is not an endless well, so the cap protects him too. It protects you because you finally know what you can count on.

2. Set the capital call rules. Decide exactly what happens when the operating account runs low. Who funds it, how much, and how fast. One clean alternative model is pro rata by ownership, where each partner funds their equity share of any raise. Pick the structure that fits your reality, then write it down.

3. Add a clawback clause. If he agrees to a capital call and then does not fund it, a defined portion of his equity transfers to you over a set time period. This is the protection you did not have this month. It turns his commitment into something real.

I will send a short follow up with suggested structures and numbers for the funding cap, the capital call mechanism, and the clawback so you have a starting point to take to your attorney.


PART 3: RUN THE BUSINESS LIKE AN OPERATOR

1. Budget by quarter. Each quarter, set the plan and review actuals against it. Decide upfront if it is a growth quarter or a profit quarter, and agree on the funding need before it happens. No surprises.

2. Cut the overhead that does not serve the business. Spend crept up on shiny objects. Strip it back to what drives clients and delivery. Add cost only when it ties to revenue.

3. Decide on an operator. If Eric will not run operations and the load is too heavy, weigh hiring a high level operator. Both paths are hard. Spend early and run tight, or do it yourself and hire later while carrying the stress. There is no easy version. Pick the balance you can live with.


PART 4: FIX THE GROWTH ENGINE

1. Reframe how you judge ads. Ads are not a yes or no on working. It comes down to cost per booked call, show rate, and how much you put in. Keep spend at a real level and optimize the cost down over time.

2. Get Jacques onto Dave's media buying call. Have him prepare two to three specific questions. He sends them to you first so you can review. Then he asks Dave directly on the call. The biggest mistake on these calls is showing up and asking nothing.

3. Have Jacques request a live campaign review. He shares the campaign on the call and asks Dave to review it with the group. You get a top media buyer reviewing your account. Use it.

4. Add a recurring client call. Your clients are less conversational than normal. Put a regular client community or collab call on the calendar so they engage, stay, and feel part of something. Keep it intimate.


YOUR NEXT 7 DAYS

1. Request and confirm access to every financial and operational account.
2. Have the conversation with Eric. He is out of operations. He keeps equity tied to a defined funding role.
3. List the overhead line by line. Mark what stays and what gets cut.
4. Tell Jacques to prep his two to three questions and send them to you before Dave's call.
5. Block a recurring client call on the calendar and announce it to clients.
6. Take the funding cap, capital call, and clawback language to your attorney once I send it over.


VEGAS

Eric and his wife are coming. Good. Get him immersed in the room so he understands this is active client work, not a passive project.
